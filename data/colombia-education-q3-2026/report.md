# Education in Emergencies for Displaced and Host-Community Children

*Northbridge Relief Colombia. Quarterly progress report, third quarter 2026 (1/7/2026 to 30/9/2026). Project code NR-CO-2026-007.*

## 1. Executive summary

Between July and September the programme enrolled 3,436 out-of-school children in accelerated learning classes in La Guajira, Arauca and Norte de Santander (101 per cent of planned enrolment). Of these learners, 1,766 were girls and 1,570 were boys. Seventy-three teachers, fifty-three of them women, completed the psychosocial support course.

Attendance is the main concern. Roughly two thirds of enrolled children attended at least 80 per cent of sessions, short of the 75 per cent benchmark agreed with the donor. Seasonal work in the coffee and palm harvests, the cost of transport and the movement of families across the border all pulled children away from class.

The programme works with the departmental education secretariats to offer accelerated learning to children who have missed years of school, either because they were displaced inside Colombia or because their families arrived from Venezuela. Classes follow the flexible education models recognised by the Ministry of Education, so that learners can be placed in the right grade of a public school once they have caught up. Alongside the classes, the programme trains teachers in psychosocial support, distributes learning materials, provides a hot meal on each school day and works with parents through school committees.

## 2. Operating environment

Arrivals from Venezuela continued through informal crossings near Cúcuta and Paraguachón. Many families arrive with children who have been out of school for two years or more, and a large share settle in informal settlements on the edge of Riohacha, Maicao and Saravena where public schools are already full. The Ministry of Education's flexible models allow these children to catch up and then move into the formal system, which is the route the programme follows.

Armed group activity in rural Arauca restricted movement for staff and families on several occasions. Classes were moved to safer community spaces when needed, and no learner or staff member was harmed. Heavy rain in September damaged roads in southern La Guajira and delayed the delivery of school meals for a week.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Access to education

Enrolment by department was as follows. La Guajira: 1,294; Arauca: 1,007; Norte de Santander: 1,135. Girls accounted for 51 per cent of all learners. The figure stood at 2,969 at the end of June, so the programme grew by more than 400 children over the quarter, almost all of them in La Guajira.

Attendance varied widely. In Arauca, where the security situation interrupted classes, only 72 per cent of enrolled children attended at least 80 per cent of sessions. In the settlements around Riohacha, attendance improved after the programme started paying for a school bus on two routes.

A total of 3,303 learning kits (notebooks, pencils, a backpack and a reading book) were handed out, 1,080 of them in Norte de Santander. The team also set up sixteen temporary learning spaces against a target of nineteen, using tents and rehabilitated community halls.

### 3.2 Quality of teaching and wellbeing

The psychosocial support course has five modules on stress, grief, classroom routines, referral and self-care. In La Guajira 30 teachers finished all the modules; the rest of the teachers were in Arauca and Norte de Santander. Only thirty men took the course, reflecting the profile of the teaching workforce in the programme.

Teachers reported that learners who had been out of school for long periods needed help with concentration and with managing conflict in class. Each learning space now has a quiet corner and a referral list for child protection services.

### 3.3 Families and school committees

Upwards of 1,050 parents and caregivers attended school committee meetings this quarter, roughly 73 per cent of them mothers or female caregivers. Committees in Maicao organised the rota for preparing school meals and helped the team trace children who had stopped attending.

School meals remain the strongest incentive to attend. 196.0 thousand hot meals were served over the quarter, 87 per cent of the plan. In Arauca, 75,066 meals were served, and in Norte de Santander the figure was 68,520.

## 4. Accountability and safeguarding

Children and parents can give feedback through suggestion boxes, a WhatsApp line (+57 300 555 0142) and monthly meetings with the school committees. Most feedback asked for more places and for classes in the afternoon. All staff and teachers signed the code of conduct and completed the safeguarding induction before working with children (see section 5.2 of the annual plan).

## 5. Inclusion

Children with disabilities are identified at enrolment through a short questionnaire completed with the parents. Teachers adapt activities for them, and the programme pays for transport where a child cannot walk to class. Indigenous Wayuu children in La Guajira are taught in mixed groups with a bilingual assistant, so that they can follow lessons in Spanish while keeping their own language.

Adolescent girls are at higher risk of leaving school because of domestic work and early pregnancy. Female teachers lead small group sessions for them on life skills, and the school committees follow up with families when a girl stops attending. Boys in border areas face recruitment risks, and the referral list in each learning space includes the child protection services that can respond to this.

## 6. Monitoring and learning

Teachers take attendance every day on paper forms, which field coordinators collect and enter into the monitoring matrix each week. Enrolment is checked against the national student registration system once a child has an identity document, so that the same child is not counted twice when families move between departments.

Learning progress is measured with a short reading and arithmetic test at enrolment and again after each cycle. The first results will be available at the end of the year, when the first group of learners completes the cycle. The team is also documenting how teachers adapt the flexible models to the reality of each settlement, to share with the Ministry of Education and other partners in the education cluster.

## 7. Coordination

The programme takes part in the education in emergencies working groups in Riohacha and Cúcuta and reports to the national education cluster each month. Locations for learning spaces were agreed with the departmental education secretariats and with other organisations running flexible models, so that services are not duplicated. Learners who complete the cycle are referred to public schools through the secretariats, which keep places for them where they can.

## 8. Challenges and lessons

Recruiting teachers for rural Arauca remains difficult because of insecurity and low pay. The programme now pairs new teachers with experienced ones for their first month. Attendance data are collected on paper and entered weekly, which delays follow-up with families; a tablet-based register will be piloted in two learning spaces next quarter.

The lesson from this quarter is that enrolment alone is not a good measure of progress. The programme will report attendance and transition to the formal system alongside enrolment from now on, and will review the 80 per cent attendance threshold with the donor.

Staff turnover was lower than in the first half of the year after the programme aligned teacher pay with the rates used by the secretariats. Field coordinators now spend one day a week in the classroom with new teachers, which has helped them manage large mixed-age groups. The programme also learned that families respond better to a home visit by a teacher they know than to a phone call from the office, and home visits will become the standard follow-up for absent learners.

Finally, the programme depends on the goodwill of community leaders who lend halls and land for learning spaces. Written agreements are now signed with each of them, setting out who maintains the space and what happens if the community needs it back during an emergency.

## 9. Outlook

In the fourth quarter the programme expects to transfer around 600 learners to formal schools at the start of the new school year in January 2027, and to open three more learning spaces in Maicao.
